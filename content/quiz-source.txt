 QUESTION 01 / PROBLEM FRAMING & DISCOVERY

A food-delivery app receives complaints that checkout is "too slow." A stakeholder
immediately asks for fewer screens. What should the designer do first?

 A. Remove the address and payment steps
 B. Inspect the checkout journey and talk to users to find where and why delays happen
 C. Add a progress animation to make waiting feel shorter
 D. Copy the checkout flow from the most popular competitor

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: The complaint describes a symptom, not its cause. Reviewing funnel data and observing users can reveal whether
 the issue is slow loading, confusing fields, payment failures or something else.
 TAKEAWAY: Problem framing: understand the cause before choosing a solution.

 QUESTION 02 / PROBLEM FRAMING & DISCOVERY

People using a budgeting app say, "I want to stop feeling surprised by bills." Which
statement best captures the underlying Job to Be Done?

 A. Add a calendar with more colors
 B. Help me anticipate upcoming expenses so I can feel in control of my money
 C. Send three push notifications every week
 D. Add a dashboard with six new charts

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: JTBD describes the progress a person wants to make in a situation. The other options jump to specific features
 before clarifying the outcome.
 TAKEAWAY: JTBD: focus on the progress people are trying to make, not the feature they request.

 QUESTION 03 / PROBLEM FRAMING & DISCOVERY

Your team wants to launch an AI shopping assistant. You are unsure whether shoppers
trust AI recommendations. What is the most useful next step before investing in a full
build?

 A. Build the complete assistant to see what happens
 B. List the key assumptions and run a small test of the riskiest trust assumption
 C. Add more AI features to make the concept impressive
 D. Ask the engineering team which model they prefer

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Assumption mapping makes uncertainty visible. Testing the riskiest assumption early helps avoid spending
 heavily on a solution users may not trust.
 TAKEAWAY: Riskiest assumption: test the uncertainty that could invalidate the idea.

 QUESTION 04 / PROBLEM FRAMING & DISCOVERY

During an interview about a new booking flow, which question is least leading and most
likely to produce useful evidence?

 A. You liked the new booking flow, right?
 B. Wouldn't a one-tap booking button save you time?
 C. Tell me about the last time you booked a similar service. What happened?
 D. Do you agree that the old flow was confusing?

 RIGHT ANSWER: C

 Why: Asking about a recent real experience encourages concrete examples. The other questions suggest an expected
 answer or ask participants to agree with the interviewer.
 TAKEAWAY: Ask about the last real experience, not what someone thinks they might do.

 QUESTION 05 / PROBLEM FRAMING & DISCOVERY

A team is debating two different onboarding concepts. Engineering estimates that building
either version will take several weeks. What is a sensible way to learn which direction is
clearer first?

 A. Build both versions fully
 B. Create lightweight clickable prototypes and test representative tasks with target users
 C. Let the loudest stakeholder choose
 D. Pick the version with the most screens

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: A prototype can test comprehension and task flow before costly implementation. It is a learning tool, not a
 miniature finished product.
 TAKEAWAY: Prototype to learn: use the lowest-cost representation that can answer the question.

 QUESTION 06 / USER RESEARCH & VALIDATION

Analytics show that 38% of users abandon a profile form. The team knows where drop-off
happens but not why. What should the designer do next?

 A. Run usability sessions or interviews focused on the form
 B. Increase the number of dashboard charts
 C. Assume users dislike the brand colors
 D. Remove every field without checking its purpose

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Quantitative data reveals the scale and location of a problem. Qualitative research helps explain the reasons
 behind the behavior.
 TAKEAWAY: Use quantitative research to see what is happening and qualitative research to explore why.

 QUESTION 07 / USER RESEARCH & VALIDATION

You are designing a medication reminder experience and need to understand how routines
change across weekdays, weekends and travel. Which method is most suitable?

 A. A one-time five-minute preference survey
 B. A diary study in which participants record relevant moments over time
 C. A card sort of navigation labels
 D. A single hallway test of the home screen

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: A diary study captures behavior and context over time, including moments that may not be remembered
 accurately in a one-off interview.
 TAKEAWAY: Diary study: capture experiences as they happen, across time and context.

 QUESTION 08 / USER RESEARCH & VALIDATION

You want to know whether first-time users can find and apply a discount code. What
instruction should you give participants during a usability test?

 A. Click the coupon button in the top-right corner
 B. Apply the coupon code SAVE10 to your order
 C. Find a way to apply this coupon to your order; please think aloud as you go
 D. Tell us whether the coupon feature looks attractive

 RIGHT ANSWER: C

 Why: A task gives participants a goal without revealing the interface path. This lets the researcher observe
 discoverability and actual behavior.
 TAKEAWAY: Test the task, not the UI instructions: do not tell participants where to click.

 QUESTION 09 / USER RESEARCH & VALIDATION

Interviews suggest users love a new feature, but analytics show very low repeat usage.
What is the best research response?

 A. Ignore analytics because interviews are more personal
 B. Ignore interviews because analytics are always correct
 C. Combine behavioral data with follow-up research to understand the gap
 D. Announce that the feature is successful because initial feedback was positive

 RIGHT ANSWER: C

 Why: Triangulation compares different evidence sources. The mismatch is a clue worth investigating, not a reason to
 discard one source.
 TAKEAWAY: Triangulation: combine methods or evidence sources to build a more reliable picture.

 QUESTION 10 / USER RESEARCH & VALIDATION

A team is redesigning a tool used by warehouse supervisors. Who should be prioritized for
research?

 A. People from the design team who have free time
 B. Friends who use shopping apps
 C. People who perform the warehouse supervisor tasks in the intended context
 D. Any available office employees

 RIGHT ANSWER: C

 Why: Research participants should reflect the people, tasks and context relevant to the product decision. Convenience
 alone does not make a sample useful.
 TAKEAWAY: Recruit for relevance: match participants to the target users and real context.

 QUESTION 11 / UX PSYCHOLOGY & INTERACTION

A travel app has 12 optional filters. New users say the search screen feels overwhelming.
Which redesign best applies Progressive Disclosure?

 A. Show essential filters first and place advanced filters behind "More options"
 B. Show every filter at once
 C. Remove all advanced filters permanently
 D. Require users to complete every filter before searching

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Progressive Disclosure reveals complexity when it becomes relevant. It keeps the initial experience focused
 without taking away advanced capabilities.
 TAKEAWAY: Designer phrase: "Let's progressively disclose advanced options to keep the default experience focused."

 QUESTION 12 / UX PSYCHOLOGY & INTERACTION

A support portal asks users to remember a six-digit case number from a previous screen.
How could the experience reduce memory effort?

 A. Make the number flash briefly before hiding it
 B. Display the case number in the current context, with a copy option
 C. Ask users to memorize it before continuing
 D. Replace it with a longer code

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Recognition is easier than recall. Showing relevant information when it is needed reduces the burden on a user's
 memory.
 TAKEAWAY: Recognition over recall: make needed choices and information visible.

 QUESTION 13 / UX PSYCHOLOGY & INTERACTION

In a shopping app, "Apply coupon" looks like ordinary body text, so people do not realize it
is clickable. What change would most clearly signal the interaction?

 A. Reduce the font size
 B. Style it as a recognizable button or link with clear interactive treatment
 C. Move it into a paragraph
 D. Use a lighter gray than surrounding text

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: A signifier is a perceivable cue that communicates where or how an action can happen. A clear button or link
 treatment makes the affordance easier to notice.
 TAKEAWAY: Signifiers: make interactive possibilities visible rather than relying on users to guess.

 QUESTION 14 / UX PSYCHOLOGY & INTERACTION

A user taps "Save" on a long form. Nothing appears to happen, so they tap repeatedly.
What should the interface do?

 A. Disable the button with no explanation
 B. Provide a clear saving state followed by a success confirmation
 C. Navigate away immediately, even if saving is incomplete
 D. Change the button color randomly

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Timely system feedback tells people that their action was received and whether it succeeded. It reduces
 uncertainty and repeated actions.
 TAKEAWAY: Visibility of system status: keep users informed about what the system is doing.

 QUESTION 15 / UX PSYCHOLOGY & INTERACTION

A user accidentally removes an item from a task list. Which response supports recovery
without interrupting every routine action?

 A. Show a brief confirmation message with an Undo action
 B. Permanently delete the item with no message
 C. Require a five-step confirmation for every edit
 D. Hide the task list after deletion

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: An Undo option offers a quick recovery path for a reversible action. This can prevent the cost and friction of
 excessive confirmation dialogs.
 TAKEAWAY: Design for recovery: make common mistakes easy to reverse.

 QUESTION 16 / INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE & FLOWS

Users disagree about which items belong under "Account," "Preferences" and "Privacy."

 QUESTION 17 / INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE & FLOWS

A settings menu has been reorganized. Before designing its visual interface, you want to
check whether people can find "Download my data" in the proposed hierarchy. What
should you run?

 C. A five-second logo test
 D. A diary study about daily routines

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Tree testing evaluates whether people can find information in a proposed hierarchy without the influence of visual
 styling or page layout.
 TAKEAWAY: Tree testing: validate findability in the structure before polishing the interface.

An online store has 2,000 headphones. Shoppers often know the features they need but

 QUESTION 20 / INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE & FLOWS

A new project-management account has no projects yet. Which empty state best helps a

 A. A completely blank page
 B. "No data" with no next step

 D. A long technical explanation of the database

 RIGHT ANSWER: C

 Why: A useful empty state explains the situation and gives the user a next step. It can turn a blank screen into guided
 onboarding.
 TAKEAWAY: Empty-state design: explain, reassure and guide the next action.

 QUESTION 21 / UI DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

 QUESTION 22 / UI DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

A product has six slightly different purple button colors across screens. The team wants
consistent updates without editing each screen individually. What should it establish?

 A. Design tokens for shared color values and semantic roles
 B. More one-off styles
 C. A screenshot folder
 D. A rule that every designer picks a favorite purple

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Design tokens store reusable design decisions such as color, spacing and typography values. Semantic tokens
 make consistent changes easier across a product.
 TAKEAWAY: Design tokens: store reusable design decisions instead of repeating raw values.

 QUESTION 23 / UI DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

A desktop dashboard uses a four-column data table. On a narrow phone, the columns
become unreadable. What is the best response?

 A. Shrink every text label until all columns fit
 B. Adapt the layout for the smaller screen, prioritizing key information and suitable mobile patterns
 C. Keep the desktop layout unchanged
 D. Remove all data from mobile

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Responsive design adapts content and interaction to the available space and context. It is not simply scaling a
 desktop layout down.
 TAKEAWAY: Responsive design: adapt the experience to the screen, not just the dimensions.

 QUESTION 24 / UI DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

A dashboard marks overdue tasks in red and completed tasks in green. Some users cannot
distinguish the colors. What should be added?

 A. More shades of red and green only
 B. Text labels or icons so status is not communicated by color alone

 D. Remove status information entirely

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Color should not be the only way information is conveyed. Additional labels, symbols or patterns make status
 perceivable to more people.
 TAKEAWAY: Redundant encoding: communicate important meaning in more than one visual way.

 QUESTION 25 / UI DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

A design system has separate button components for every color and size combination.
Updates are becoming inconsistent. What is a better model?

 A. Create a reusable button component with clearly defined variants and states
 B. Duplicate every button on every screen
 C. Stop documenting button behavior
 D. Use screenshots instead of components

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: A component with controlled variants and states provides reuse while allowing legitimate differences, such as
 primary, secondary, disabled and loading.
 TAKEAWAY: Component variants: reuse a reliable base while documenting meaningful differences.

 QUESTION 26 / ACCESSIBILITY & INCLUSIVE DESIGN

A modal dialog opens, but keyboard users can still tab through links behind it. What should
the team fix?

 A. Trap focus appropriately within the modal while it is open, and return focus when it closes
 B. Remove the close button
 C. Make the modal open only on hover
 D. Hide the visible focus indicator

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: A modal dialog should manage keyboard focus so users can interact with its content without accidentally moving
 behind it. Focus should return to the invoking control when appropriate.
 TAKEAWAY: Focus management: keep keyboard navigation in the active task context.

 QUESTION 27 / ACCESSIBILITY & INCLUSIVE DESIGN

A product uses pale gray text on a white background for important instructions. Some
users struggle to read it. What should be checked first?

 A. Whether the font is decorative
 B. Text and background contrast against accessibility requirements
 C. Whether the page has enough animations
 D. Whether the text is centered

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Insufficient contrast can make text difficult to perceive. Check the actual foreground and background contrast
 and adjust the colors while preserving the intended visual hierarchy.
 TAKEAWAY: Contrast check: visual subtlety should not come at the cost of readability.

 QUESTION 28 / ACCESSIBILITY & INCLUSIVE DESIGN

A sign-up form uses placeholder text as the only label for each input. Once users start
typing, the hints disappear. What is the better pattern?

 A. Keep a persistent visible label associated with each input
 B. Remove all labels
 C. Use a different placeholder color for every field
 D. Put all field names in the page footer

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Persistent labels remain available while users enter data and provide a reliable name for assistive technologies
 when correctly associated with inputs.
 TAKEAWAY: Persistent labels: do not make users rely on disappearing hints to understand a field.

 QUESTION 29 / ACCESSIBILITY & INCLUSIVE DESIGN

Users frequently miss a tiny close icon in a mobile dialog. What should the designer
consider?

 A. Increase the interactive target area and provide enough separation from nearby controls
 B. Make the icon thinner
 C. Move it closer to other small icons
 D. Remove the accessible name

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Larger, well-spaced touch targets are easier to activate, especially for users with motor impairments or when
 using a device one-handed.
 TAKEAWAY: Touch target: design for fingers and real-world conditions, not just the visible icon.

 QUESTION 30 / ACCESSIBILITY & INCLUSIVE DESIGN

An automated accessibility checker reports no serious issues. Can the team conclude the
flow is accessible?

 A. Yes, automated tools detect every accessibility problem
 B. No. Combine automated checks with keyboard, assistive-technology and user testing
 C. Yes, if the design uses a popular font
 D. No. Automated tools should never be used

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Automated tools catch many detectable issues but cannot evaluate every aspect of understanding, task
 completion or assistive-technology behavior.
 TAKEAWAY: Accessibility is a practice: automate what you can and test the experience with people.

 QUESTION 31 / PRODUCT THINKING & PRIORITIZATION

A major customer asks for a customizable dashboard. Interviews reveal that their real
difficulty is finding two critical reports quickly. What should the product designer do?

 A. Build the customizable dashboard exactly as requested
 B. Explore the report-finding problem and test simpler ways to make those reports easier to access
 C. Reject the request without investigation
 D. Add every report to the home screen

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Feature requests are clues, not complete requirements. Understanding the underlying job may reveal a simpler
 solution that serves more users.
 TAKEAWAY: Reframe the request: solve the user problem, not automatically the suggested feature.

 QUESTION 32 / PRODUCT THINKING & PRIORITIZATION

A startup wants to test whether local stores will accept online reservations. Which option
is the best MVP approach?

 A. Build a complete marketplace with loyalty, reviews and recommendations
 B. Test the core reservation journey with a small number of stores and customers
 C. Spend the entire budget polishing the logo
 D. Wait until every possible feature is specified

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: A Minimum Viable Product is the smallest useful product or experiment that can test an important assumption
 and generate learning. It is not simply the smallest number of features.
 TAKEAWAY: MVP: minimize the build while maximizing validated learning.

 QUESTION 33 / PRODUCT THINKING & PRIORITIZATION

Your team has capacity for one improvement. Option A is requested by one executive but
has little evidence. Option B addresses a frequent user problem and is aligned with the
product goal. What should guide the decision?

 A. Seniority of the person requesting the feature
 B. Evidence of user impact, strategic alignment, effort and risk
 C. Which option has the more exciting name
 D. Whichever option is easiest to present

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Prioritization should make trade-offs explicit. User impact, strategy, confidence, effort and risk provide a stronger
 basis than hierarchy or enthusiasm alone.
 TAKEAWAY: Prioritization is a trade-off: compare value, evidence, effort and strategic fit.

 QUESTION 34 / PRODUCT THINKING & PRIORITIZATION

Users love a proposed service and find it easy to use. However, each transaction costs the
company more to deliver than it earns. Which product risk is most clearly unresolved?

 B. Value risk

 TAKEAWAY: Product viability: a product must create value in a way the business can sustain.

 B. A mood board
 C. A site map alone
 D. A list of engineering estimates

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: An Opportunity Solution Tree connects a desired outcome to customer opportunities, possible solutions and
 experiments. It helps teams avoid jumping straight from a metric to a feature.
 TAKEAWAY: Opportunity Solution Tree: connect outcomes to customer needs, solutions and tests.

 QUESTION 36 / METRICS & EXPERIMENTATION

A redesigned checkout increases the number of users who click "Pay," but payment
failures also rise. What should the team do?

 A. Declare success based on button clicks
 B. Review the primary outcome together with guardrail metrics such as successful payments and errors

 B. Protect the core user journey and essential states, then defer lower-value enhancements
 C. Keep every feature but remove usability testing
 D. Ship incomplete payment and error flows

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Scope reduction should preserve the product's essential value and safe completion of core tasks. Lower-priority
 enhancements can be staged for later releases.
 TAKEAWAY: Scope the release around the core journey, not around a random feature count.

 QUESTION 46 / ETHICS, PRIVACY & RESPONSIBLE AI

A subscription app makes signing up easy but hides cancellation under several confusing
menus. What is the most responsible design response?

 A. Make cancellation equally clear and reasonably easy to find
 B. Add more steps to discourage cancellation
 C. Rename cancellation as an unrelated settings option
 D. Use guilt-based messages to stop users leaving

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: A design should not manipulate users into staying. Clear, accessible cancellation supports user autonomy and
 trust.
 TAKEAWAY: Avoid dark patterns: do not use interface friction to undermine informed choice.

 QUESTION 47 / ETHICS, PRIVACY & RESPONSIBLE AI

A photo-editing app asks for camera access immediately on launch, before the user tries to
take a photo. What is a better approach?

 A. Ask at the moment the user tries to use the camera and explain why access is needed
 B. Ask for every permission at first launch
 C. Hide the purpose of the permission request
 D. Block the entire app until every permission is granted

 RIGHT ANSWER: A

 Why: Contextual permission requests explain the value at the moment it is relevant. This supports informed choice and
 can improve comprehension.
 TAKEAWAY: Just-in-time permission: ask when needed and explain the benefit clearly.

 QUESTION 48 / ETHICS, PRIVACY & RESPONSIBLE AI

An AI assistant recommends a financial action, but its confidence is low and the user could
lose money. What should the product do?

 A. Present the recommendation as certain to encourage action
 B. Communicate uncertainty and limitations, provide relevant context and keep the user in control of the decision
 C. Hide that AI generated the recommendation
 D. Automatically execute the action without confirmation

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: High-impact recommendations need appropriate transparency, uncertainty communication and user control. The
 interface should not imply confidence that the system does not have.
 TAKEAWAY: Responsible AI: make limitations visible and preserve meaningful human control.

 QUESTION 49 / ETHICS, PRIVACY & RESPONSIBLE AI

A simple event-booking app asks for a user's exact date of birth, home address and
employer, even though none is needed to book a ticket. What should the designer
recommend?

 A. Collect everything in case it becomes useful later
 B. Collect only the data needed for a clear purpose and explain that purpose to users
 C. Make all fields mandatory
 D. Hide the data collection in the terms page

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: Data minimization limits collection to what is necessary for the stated purpose. Less unnecessary data reduces
 privacy risk and builds trust.
 TAKEAWAY: Data minimization: if the product does not need the data, do not collect it.

 QUESTION 50 / ETHICS, PRIVACY & RESPONSIBLE AI

An AI assistant drafts a message to a customer and can send it automatically. The message
may include pricing and delivery commitments. What is the safer interaction?

 A. Send every generated message immediately
 B. Show the draft, highlight important details and ask the user to review and confirm before sending
 C. Hide the generated text after creation
 D. Remove the ability to edit the message

 RIGHT ANSWER: B

 Why: When an AI action can create a meaningful external consequence, review and confirmation give people a chance
 to catch errors and retain control.
 TAKEAWAY: Human-in-the-loop: keep people involved when automated actions have real consequences.
